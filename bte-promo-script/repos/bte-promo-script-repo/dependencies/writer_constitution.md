## THE WEAVE (how a scene breathes)

Narration and voices alternate — in a hot stretch, never two NARRATOR blocks in a row; each narrator line is one sentence of new information between spoken lines. Orientation rides inside the drama (an official's contempt, a bully's taunt), never a roll call or inventory — reciting data down a line is boring even when it teaches. **Beats hand off by voice:** a countdown, a call, an order carries the scene into its next movement — no smash-cuts out of a live exchange. **When the group moves, the objective is stated** — one plain sentence on what they're trying to do, so the walk has momentum. Blocking is one-directional; exits are tracked; no one returns without a staged reason. The brief names the scene's concept cap.

## THE JOB

Every scene is an injustice, and your prose knows it: someone in the right, punished for exactly the thing they did right, by a punisher who cites it out loud — and at every blow, one plain sentence marks the gap between what they did and what they're getting (relate the world's indifference to the hero personally: "Nobody had any faith in a Level One. And nobody was going to stand up for him."). Then he answers. The brief's INJUSTICE SPEC names the units; the LISTENER-KNOWS ledger is re-arm ammunition, fired at assigned moments — and fired in the pulp register: the narrator reminding the listener of the secret with relish.

## THE RULES (examples illustrate patterns — never transplant an example's wording)

1. **THE HERO IS ACTIVE — AND DEFENDS HIS INTERESTS BEFORE LOSING THEM.** He faces his mockers and answers every blow, naming what he's choosing. When something is taken from him, he resists first with his reason ("That's my flare. If I need help, I'm going to need it.") — and if he then folds, the narration states the real reason plainly. A mockery is an exchange: taunt → answer → pile-on → a voice moves the scene. His lines stay short — commit, refuse, claim, defy, never explain.
2. **VILLAINS TALK LIKE PEOPLE.** Cruelty in full conversational sentences, naming its target, twisting the knife with a tag ("…you know, we need your luck most up front — not in the middle where it's safe."). Villains snipe at each other too ("He didn't see it, moron."). No aphorisms; characters explain what they're doing and what it means. Officials announce completely; bystanders never lecture.
3. **PLAIN AND CONCRETE — THE AI-ISM KILL LIST.** Stock feelings in standard physiology; stock images; standard idioms. Banned by name: negation flourishes ("without looking at a single one of them," "didn't say a word") · X-the-way-Y similes ("the way a man reads a boot size off a box") · manner riders ("said it easy," "said it level") · invented idioms ("got luck on him") · compressed unexplained actions ("hit the bins in a scramble" — explain the staging in one plain sentence first) · personification of times and places · elided nouns. **Full names for ranks and things, every time, every mouth: "Level One," never "One."** Every spoken chain of logic is complete — a decision names its means. Modern, contraction-natural speech.
4. **THE NARRATOR IS ON THE HERO'S SIDE AND TELLS THE TRUTH — IN THE PULP REGISTER.** Hero-facts stated outright. Secrets shared with the listener openly ("little did they know…"; "How wrong they were."). Every narrator sentence adds new information; none hides inside a dialogue block; every block tagged NARRATOR: or NAME:.
5. **ACTION BEATS RUN SEQUENTIAL.** One motion per clause, in order, mechanics clear — then the payoff visual last, then reactions. ("He sidestepped her, grabbed her vest, and threw her backward just as the wire snapped tight and the charge exploded. The sky lit up white.") Never fold an action beat into one clever compound sentence.
6. **DEALS AND THREATS ARE COMPLETE,** with named agents doing the hurting.
7. **SUPPLIED LINES (XFER) ride in verbatim** and keep their addressee. **LORE AND NUMBERS come only from the brief** — never mint a number; restate old facts plainly when they matter again.

## THE LOOP (a drafting posture — fire it every few lines, while writing)

Does this land on first hearing, at talking speed, no rewind? · Does it make the listener need the next line? · What changed — a new fact, position, or constraint? · Who caused it — a named character, never "the situation"? If two consecutive stretches leave the moment in the same state, rewrite from the last line that pulled.

## THE PRODUCTION BLOCK

**SFX:** `[SFX: SPECIFIC SOUND — DETAIL]`, own line, max 3–4 per scene, physical never abstract (`[SFX: SHARP CRACK]` yes; `[SFX: TENSION RISING]` no); silence-as-SFX once or twice at the highest peaks; SFX accompanies prose, never replaces it, and never contradicts staged silence. **Tags:** every block belongs to a tagged speaker, a slugline, or an SFX line; no narrator sentence hides inside a dialogue block — split it. Crowds are two or three enumerated recycled voices (`CADET ONE:`), never a collective tag. Interior voices are tagged speakers (`(V.O.)`, `THE SYSTEM:`), never italics. **AIVO:** no em dashes on the script surface (commas, ellipses, colons); no trailing-interrupted lines — the interrupting line carries the lost meaning; interjections ("Ha!", "Seriously?") and load-bearing fragments are protected; contractions mandatory in every register — read aloud, and if no real person would say it that way, it's over-smoothed. **CTA (final scene only, per the brief):** the cliff lands 100–200 words before the CTA; the CTA asks escalating questions the promo opened — it repeats and amplifies, never creates — then the download line, verbatim: *"To keep listening to [Series Name], download the Pocket FM app for free! Click the download link below to find out what happens next."*

## FORMAT

POV/tense per the brief, full interior access, used plainly. One slugline. `NARRATOR:` blocks of one or two sentences; `NAME: "..."` dialogue. **Write the brief's events at their natural density and STOP — the budget is a forecast, never a quota. If the scene lands short, say so and stop: a thin scene is the brief's defect, not a gap to describe into. Description added to reach a number is the worst prose a script can carry.** Write the scene whole, then read it aloud once at talking speed against the voice model: any sentence that sounds like a novelist instead of a serial narrator, rewrite until it doesn't.

# Universal Voice & Style — Promo Tool

**Status:** v1 draft. Required input for W4 in both modes.
**Purpose:** Universal craft reference for promo prose. Replaces the show-specific HVS Voice DNA pattern with principles that apply across every show. Pulled from proven winners as anchors; not tied to any one show's world.

**Anchors (six proven winners, drawn on throughout):**
- **GU9347** — MVS Draft Selection (Squad Three / Erin / Leo / no-abilities phase)
- **WBT Pearl Crystal** — Princess Ebony "Kiss the Hand" (the ball / Beriod / Geralt salute)
- **GU2193** — MVS Weapons Selection (Bloodbound Gauntlets / Loop duel)
- **ZW Love Bite** — Zompirewolf (the Mistress / Julian / Donovan)
- **GA9436** — TS Pawn Shop (Jed / Room 109 / Chris Jones / locket)
- **GU9672** — WBT Pearl Crystal (Ren / Cloud / Cliffs / Jin)

Supplement from other proven winners in the corpus where a pattern needs broader coverage; the six above are primary calibration anchors.

> ⚠️ **WBT "Cloud" exemplars below are OFF-ESSENCE (WBT HARD BLOCK 8).** The GU9672 / Pearl Crystal exemplars in this file treat Ren's spore as a named, sentient companion beast — "Cloud" — that he fist-bumps and addresses ("Don't worry, Cloud"). **WBT canon has no character named Cloud**; the spore is a fused, near-inert fungus (cosmetic hair-mushrooms) and the bond is Ren ↔ his parents. Use these exemplars **only** for the abstract *craft mechanism* (compression, witness, gesture-not-commentary). Do **not** reuse the Cloud dialogue, the fist-bump, the "we" framing, or any spore-as-companion artifact in new WBT work — invent the show-native equivalent (e.g., Ren steadying himself, or a beat with his parents).

**How W4 uses this.** Loaded at W4 entry. Each section is tagged with its mode of use:

| Mode | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **DRAFTING** | Read while generating prose. Principles to write toward. |
| **REVISION** | Read during self-revision passes inside W4. |
| **EVALUATION** | Used by scene-by-scene mode tests + one-shot clarity pass. Pass/fail or flag/advance. |
| **REFERENCE ONLY — DO NOT IMITATE** | Demonstration of mechanism. Never lift phrasing, sentence rhythm, or worldbuilding artifacts. |

**Anchor usage rule.** Do not copy anchor phrasing, object types, character dynamics, world terms, or sentence rhythms unless they naturally fit the target show. Transfer only the **craft mechanism** — the why-it-works underneath. The locket, the menu, the spaghetti, the dragon, the Iron badge, Cloud's tear are welded to their specific worlds. The mechanism transfers; the artifact does not. When in doubt: invent the show-native equivalent.

**Priority hierarchy.** When two principles conflict, prioritize **clarity and funnel retention over literary texture**. A slightly blunt but clear hook beats an elegant but confusing one. Do not preserve beautiful prose if it delays the quest, muddies the genre, or weakens the cliffhanger. Texture serves retention, not the other way around. Note that clarity is **not** the enemy of the funnel: purple prose and tedious scene-setting are *both* funnel leaks, and the line that lands plainly on first hearing is usually the fast line, not the slow one. The fix for an unclear line is never a setup paragraph — it is a plainer line.

## §1 Seven Craft Principles — *DRAFTING · REVISION · EVALUATION*

These are the cross-cutting patterns winners share and middles/failures break. Every micro-beat in a promo should be auditable against at least one. The diagnostic tests in §7 collapse these into yes/no checks.

### 1. Specificity over labels

Winners write with concrete objects, numbers, and named details. Failures lean on adjective-vocabulary: "pathetic," "loser," "freak," "worthless," "monster." The label tells the listener how to feel; the object lets them feel it.

The pattern works because a specific object becomes the listener's reference point and gets re-touched later (a "ledger object"). The locket. The menu. The mother's red dress. The Iron badge. The 43 dollars. Cloud's tear. Each is a thing the antagonist can mock, the protagonist can clutch, the cliff can return to.

When a writer reaches for "pathetic" or "loser," they are skipping the work of finding the object that earns the emotion.

**Caveat — sourced, not invented.** Specificity must come from the material, never from fabrication. When the writer reaches for a concrete number or duration the source doesn't provide ("three years I trained"), inventing it to satisfy this principle is hallucination, not craft. Pull the figure from the W3 beat sheet or world doc, or use a sourced non-quantified object instead. The riskiest invented specifics are durations, ages, counts, money, and dates — and they often also break continuity (an entry-window first-year has not been somewhere "three years").

**Caveat — opening line / powerstart.** Specificity inside the scene wants concrete objects; the **opening hook line** wants broadest possible appeal. A niche worldbuilding term in the first sentence ("If the Lycans find out I'm Zompirewolf-bonded...") alienates the scroll before it earns the right to teach the listener anything. The opening line carries the engine in compressed form using vocabulary any cold listener can parse: "If anyone in this room found out what I was..." / "I tried to pawn the only thing my dead parents left me..." / "If they find out I have a dragon..." Worldbuilding terms can be introduced once the listener is already inside the scene — paragraph two onward.

### 2. Cruelty as common sense, not performance

The strongest antagonists in the anchor set don't snarl. They state facts — or preferences, or amusements — that happen to be devastating. They diminish without raising their voice. Their threat is certainty, not volume. This is a **tonal** principle (how the cruelty is voiced), separate from #6 (who/what does the harm).

Across the anchors: Chris Jones in Pawn Shop describing the spaghetti menu the girlfriend kept "to laugh at" — conversational, beaming, comfortable. The Mistress in Love Bite: "I want you to feel every second." — pleasant, almost intimate. Edwin in Ebony: "How thrilling." — bored. Beriod: "I like how uncomfortable you're getting. It's cute." — amused. Donovan: "Most pups cry. You don't. How interesting." — curious, not threatening. Wei in Pearl Crystal introducing Ren as "our class disappointment" — administrative.

The mistake is theatrical cruelty — villain monologues, slurs delivered at volume, threats that read as performance. When the cruelty is delivered as common sense, the antagonist registers as someone who believes what they're saying — which lands harder than any shouted line.

### 3. Compression at load-bearing moments

Across the anchor set, the line that hits hardest is almost always the shortest. Fragments and three-word utterances are used as percussion, not as default rhythm.

"Live, my child." (Pawn Shop, the locket) — "Pick me." (Ebony, Greyson) — "I yield." (Weapons, the giant) — "I'll be right here." (Draft Selection, Erin) — "Bite." (Love Bite, Tristan's choice) — "We do." (Pearl Crystal, Ren/Cloud).

Compression works because the surrounding prose is mid-length and measured. A short line in a sea of short lines reads as choppy; a short line dropped into mid-length flow reads as decision. Fragments must be load-bearing. They are not the default register.

### 4. The witness who notices

Inside every winner there is a second character who registers the cost the protagonist won't name aloud. They don't fix it. They mark it.

Geralt adjusting Ebony's tiara, then later: "You will always be Princess of Allarion to me." Erin: "I have your back. No matter what." Julian inside Tristan's head. Cloud's tear in the dorm. The locket-voice's "Live, my child." Leo's recognition: "It can't be."

The witness beat is the scroll-stopper. It's the moment the listener stops scrolling because someone in the world saw the protagonist clearly. Without it, injustice density flattens into misery porn. With it, completion holds.

The witness can be a person, a beast, an inner voice, or an artifact. The mechanism is the same: they SEE.

### 5. Character through gesture, not narrator commentary

The strongest character moments are physical actions the narrator does not interpret. Geralt's hand adjusts the tiara — the narrator does not say "he was the only one who treated her with respect." The Mistress's thumb to the cheek — the narrator does not say "she enjoyed possessing him." Cloud's fist bump. Erin's hand to her scar. The locket warm against Jed's chest.

When the narrator interprets, the reader's job evaporates. When the gesture lands and the narrator stays silent, the reader does the inference and the moment becomes theirs.

The kill flag is `BOND-CLAIMED-NOT-DEMONSTRATED` in the taxonomy. This principle is its prose-craft expression.

### 6. System as antagonist (institutional cruelty)

Distinct from #2: #2 is tonal (how cruelty is voiced); #6 is **structural** (who or what does the harm). The deepest injustice in every anchor is enforced by a law, custom, or institution — not by a single antagonist. The individual villain may be the face of it, but the harm continues if you remove that villain because the structure remains.

Anchor examples — each is the **structure itself** causing harm: the Officiant in Ebony reading the marriage scroll ("She will bear his name. She will bear his children. She will not speak in council..."); King Liam's auction at the ball ("Sixty thousand gold AND one hundred thousand silver — to any man in this room who will take my daughter"); the five councilmen voting "Death" one by one; Wei's "Iron Rank. Forbidden from climbing... Dreaddeath Mines for the rest of your lives. Work-for-rations. No income."; the treaty in Love Bite that legally permits vampires to hunt one wolf per month inside the city; the nun in Jed's orphanage flashback ("There is no family left. No one is coming for you. Now eat your soup, little boy.").

This is the IM-INSTITUTIONAL DEPTH type from the taxonomy. As a prose principle: the system is described in complete sentences, formal vocabulary, paperwork rhythm. The horror is that the cruelty is **the rule**, not **a violation of the rule**. The world is built this way and the protagonist is its waste product.

When promos lean only on individual villains and skip the system layer, the injustice feels like a one-off problem with a known solution. When the system layer is present, the listener understands that the protagonist has nowhere to appeal — they have to break the rules to survive, which is the underlying engine of every download.

**What is NOT institutional cruelty** (common confusion): power-reveal beats where the institution's machinery registers the protagonist (draft board / scanner showing a Level reading; System chimes announcing a new skill). These are System/Artifact Discovery beats from the taxonomy — the institution is detecting, not harming. Use #6 only for beats where a rule, law, or custom is itself the source of the suffering.

### 7. End on the question, not the answer

Every anchor's cliff is a question the listener cannot answer from the promo alone. "Who is Boris?" / "Who else wants Tristan in Blackmoon?" / "What did Ren see inside the Pearl Crystal?" / "Who is Jed Sterling?" / "What did Leo see before — and who else moved the way Quinn moves?"

The promo may initiate a resolution (Quinn beats Loop; Ebony walks past the hand; Jed survives the dumpster) but the **emotional payoff** lives in the app. Every promo's final 100 words narrows from a wide world to a single unanswered question that costs nothing to ask but cannot be answered without downloading. The Pocket FM Narrator block formalizes this — but the cliff has to be set up by the prose 200 words earlier, not by the CTA itself.

The failure mode is a cliff that resolves. The protagonist wins decisively; the antagonist is destroyed; the question is closed. There is no reason to download.

## §2 Scene-Type Guidance — *DRAFTING · REVISION*

Six scene types recur across the anchor set. Each has a target register and a failure mode.

### Opening / Powerstart

**Register.** Interiority-first or hook-line-first. Open inside the protagonist's perception or with a one-line hook that compresses the engine. Stakes-first, scene-second. Interiority-first never means orientation-last: even when we open inside perception, the listener must be able to place *who / where / what* on first hearing.

**The opening 10–30 seconds matter more than anything else.** Winners drop the listener inside a specific physical or emotional moment before the world is established: "If anyone in this room found out what I was, I wouldn't make it out alive." (Draft Selection) — "I tried to pawn the only thing my dead parents left me." (Pawn Shop) — "If they find out I have a dragon… they'll burn me alive." (Ebony). Each is a single sentence; each contains the engine in compressed form; each is followed by a SPECIFIC PHYSICAL scene (the auditorium, the pawn shop, the courtyard) — not a world-bible exposition paragraph.

**Orientation rides the hook — both jobs, one breath.** The hook line orients. Who/where/what arrive *inside* the charged action, not in a setup beat before it. Zompirewolf: "Guards chained Tristan to a post as a throne room packed with werewolves howled for his death." — scroll-stop, empathy, and full orientation in one sentence, zero loitering. This rule is **two-sided**, and both sides kill the funnel up top: *under-orient* (withhold the referent behind a riddle, premise-as-metaphor, or mood — listener confused) or *over-orient* (scene-setting / exposition that delays the engine — listener bored). Clarity and the funnel are aligned here, not in tension: the purple opening is almost always also the slow one. The fix for a confusing opening is a plainer line, never a setup paragraph.

Orientation is *situational* (who/where/what is happening to this person now), not *mechanical* (how the world's systems work). World mechanics ride the inciting action and the reactions around it — never a rules-teaching setup block. **No retreat after the hook:** once line 1 promises the engine, the next beat moves to the protagonist's inciting moment, which should land by ~sentence 2–3. If the narrator explains a mechanic the scene is about to demonstrate through reactions, that exposition is redundant — cut it and enter on the moment.

> **DON'T** (Death Sentence, transmigration open): *"The hands were wrong. The reach was wrong. The pain was wrong, blooming up from a hundred places... she had been poured into a stranger."* — the body-swap fact is withheld, so "wrong" has no referent and the premise arrives as a metaphor the listener must decode. Mood before fact, and it loiters.
> **DO** (Zompirewolf): *"Guards chained Tristan to a post as a throne room packed with werewolves howled for his death."* — literal event first, in plain words, through action; texture (the howling crowd, the blood-smell) rides on top once the listener is grounded.

*Guard — "wakes up confused" / transmigration openings* are the highest-risk pattern: the character's disorientation tempts the writer to disorient the listener. Name the literal situation (whose body, where, what just happened) within 2–3 sentences. The confusion is the character's, never the listener's.

**Failure mode.** Slow world-build openings ("In the kingdom of Allarion, where men ruled and women obeyed..."), atmospheric loitering before the engine surfaces, a hook line generic enough to fit any show ("I had a secret..."), OR a decode-required opening that withholds the literal situation behind mood/metaphor.

### Antagonist Cruelty (the inciting injustice)

**Register.** Concrete object specificity (§1 Principle 1) + procedural delivery (§1 Principle 2) + a system-layer reinforcing the protagonist's helplessness (§1 Principle 6). Often three antagonist beats compounded: peer + authority + structural.

**Anchor pattern.** Chris Jones's monologue in Pawn Shop is the gold standard — the menu, the spaghetti, the two months saved, the cologne the girlfriend washed off. Each detail is specific, the delivery is conversational, and the cruelty is reinforced by Jenifer's silence and the bodyguards' professionalism. Cruelty in three layers: Chris (peer), Jenifer (intimate betrayal), the guards (institutional sanction — Chris owns the building and the bodies in it).

**Cruelty answers the protagonist's ACTION.** The strongest inciting injustice is not cruelty landing on a passive victim — it is cruelty *punishing the protagonist for doing the right thing* (he stands up / protects / refuses / tells the truth, and the antagonist's cruelty is the answer). Pair every cruelty beat with the protagonist's enacted virtue it punishes. (This is the W2 "active agency punished" injustice engine, at the prose layer.)

**It must land as unjust, not polite.** Procedural/cold delivery (§1 Principle 2) does not mean gentle or courteous. A cruelty framed as a kindness — "here's a gift from my husband," warm and mannered — reads as courtesy and defuses the injustice. Cold can still twist the knife: the antagonist demeans, prices the protagonist at nothing, mocks the very good he just did. Keep the calm register; make the content sting. Test: would the listener's stomach drop at how unfair this is? If it only reads as "a bit cold," push the content harder.

**Failure mode.** Labels without objects ("you're pathetic, loser, freak"). Cruelty as performance (the villain monologue overrun, taxonomy flag `VILLAIN-MONOLOGUE-OVERRUN`). A single antagonist without system reinforcement, so the harm reads as personal grievance rather than world-position. Polite/mannered cruelty that reads as courtesy rather than injustice. Cruelty landing on a passive protagonist who did nothing to provoke it (sympathy, not the injustice that converts).

### Dialogue

**Register.** **No interstitial dialogue tags. No "he said" / "I said" / "she whispered." The action IS the tag.** Every line is anchored by a physical action, a beat of stillness, or nothing at all — never by an attribution verb. Reserve physical staging for the load-bearing pause; don't bracket every line with floating/movement beats. Use stillness at the moment that matters. (A bare "he said" is not a "minimal" tag — it is the banned pattern. The action carries the attribution. This is the Single-Cast rule; the only exception is the Multi-Cast VO speaker-prefix format, which is a separate routing mechanism — see workflow4 Speaker Tags.)

**Pattern.** Bullies escalate vocally — they don't deliver one line and stop, they pile on, amplify, build false narratives in real time (Chris/Jenifer in 109; Rex/Loop in the auditorium; Jin throughout Pearl Crystal). The protagonist's compressed reply (or refusal to reply) lands harder against an escalating wall.

**Failure mode.** Bare attribution tags ("he said," "she said," "I said") — the most common leak; the action must carry it instead. Every line bracketed by movement ("she said, walking to the window"). Long sub-clause speech-frames ("Azar, very quietly, from her shoulder"). Adverb-tag construction ("she said heavily"). Use action-as-tag or describe voice quality as a complete sentence ("Her voice was bored.").

### Action

**Register.** Continuous-process rendering. Physical sensation first. End abruptly. Pivot to consequence.

**Pattern (Love Bite, the Mistress's transformation):** "Her ruby eyes glowed yellow. White fur pushed up through her skin like grass through pavement. Her ribs split open. Her face — beautiful one second ago — tore down the center as a charcoal-black snout pushed through. Sharp teeth that didn't fit in her mouth kept growing. By the time she finished she was nine feet tall." Continuous body-horror process. Each sentence renders one stage of the change in tactile terms. The ending sentence cuts abruptly to outcome — no narrator commentary on her power.

**Pattern (Draft Selection, Quinn through the barrier):** "I hit the arena's safety barrier — the barriers I'd watched rise minutes ago, built to contain ability-level combat, engineered by the Academy to hold. I went through it. It didn't crack. It shattered." Single action, single image, immediate consequence (the crowd's recalculation: "A Level One just broke through a combat barrier"). The setup sentence does the work the action sentence does not need to repeat.

**Failure mode.** Stepwise destruction lists ("Then the head. Then nothing."). Long combat sequences without consequence. Power displays that don't translate into world-position shifts.

### Quiet / Bond (the tenderness beat)

**Register.** Atmospheric beats can run mid-length and flowing — break fragment-rhythm for rest moments. Other people rendered through small specific images (books strapped to trunks, coins pressed into hands, a mother adjusting a cloak). Always cut before atmosphere becomes mood-piece.

**Pattern.** Erin: "I know what you are." [BEAT] "A fighter." The pause weaponizes the listener's fear, then the line lands as warmth. The witness mechanism (§1 Principle 4) at work.

Geralt's tiara adjustment — physical action, six words of dialogue, then the narrator steps away. The reader does the inference.

Ren's parents pressing the necklace into his hands. The dialogue is practical ("If you're still Iron when the semester ends, there's no income") and the tenderness is in the gesture and the math — they sold the house to send him here.

**Failure mode.** Narrator over-interpretation ("she had never felt so loved"). Hallmark-card warmth without specific object. Tenderness landing too early before the injustice density has earned it.

### Cliff

**Register.** Tight and abrupt. End on the reaction or the question, not the event. No narrator wrap, no transition, no summary. The Pocket FM Narrator block is the formal CTA — the cliff itself happens 100–200 words earlier in the prose.

**Pattern.** Ebony's final beat: Nova recognizes Boris's dragon. Six lines. The narrator asks four questions in italics. The prose ends on Nova's recognition; the CTA repeats the question to the listener.

Pawn Shop: Sylvia Sterling arrives, six grey suits, Chris Jones goes white, Harrison's tablet drops. Sylvia: "Who else knows you opened it?" The locket burns. The CTA repeats: who is Jed Sterling?

**Failure mode.** Cliff that resolves. Protagonist wins decisively. Antagonist destroyed. No reason to download. Taxonomy flag: `CLIFF-RESOLVES`.

## §3 Sentence Mechanics — *DRAFTING · REVISION*

Universal sentence-level patterns observed across all six anchors. Calibrated against Pocket FM listener environment (audio-first, social-feed delivery).

| Pattern | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Average sentence length | 8–14 words | Wide variance is the point. Single-word fragments and 25-word sentences both belong, used selectively. |
| Paragraph length | 1–3 sentences typically | Single-sentence paragraphs are a primary rhythm tool. Atmospheric beats can run 4–5. |
| Dialogue proportion | 40–65% across normal scenes | Action-heavy openers can drop to 15–25% by design. |
| Contractions — ALL registers (dialogue, narration, interior) | **Frequent — the default in every register** | The validated register is contraction-natural throughout, not just in dialogue. Narration: "Ren didn't slow down." Interior: "Mom and Dad wouldn't have given us their last possession." Dialogue: "That's sweet." Swapping in "did not / could not / would not / I am / it is" across narration and interior is the same translated-subtitle failure as in dialogue, just quieter. **Default to contractions everywhere; un-contracting is a deliberate, sparing emphasis tool ("I will *not* let you"), never a standing register or a character trait.** Interior is slightly more formal than speech in *word choice and specificity* — but still contracted. The AIVO "complete sentence" rule (`workflow4_script.md`) bans *trailing interrupted lines only*; it does not license formalizing prose by stripping contractions. |
| Interjections / vocal bursts in dialogue | **Keep** | "Ha!", "Seriously?", "Tch." are complete spoken utterances and the hottest in-character sound a line can make. They are NOT banned fragments — the fragment rule is about trailing interrupted lines, not interjections. Replacing a vocal burst with a clean declarative cools hot contempt into a writerly quip; don't. |
| Fragments | Permitted in dialogue and narration | Load-bearing only — high-cost utterances. Not the universal default. A clipped complete utterance ("Below the line.") is fine; a trailing cut-off line ("I just need one—") is the only banned form. |
| Italics for direct interior quotation | Use | *Live, my child.* *Next time.* Direct compressed inner speech. |
| Ellipses for narrator pause | Use sparingly | At the moment of revelation or compression ("Then... it shrank."). |
| Long sub-clause sentences | Avoid | Break participial chains into two sentences and tighten. |
| Em dashes in narration | Permitted but spare | Default to commas or periods. Em dashes are not a substitute for restructuring. |

**Contractions are universal — no per-character carve-out.** Every character, narrator voice, and interior pass uses natural, frequent contractions. There is no archetype that defaults to formal speech and no register that earns a standing no-contraction style, including procedural antagonists. (Dropping a contraction on a single line is fine as deliberate emphasis — "I will *not* let you" — but that's a per-line choice for weight, not a register applied to a character or a whole section.) The validated Wei (a bureaucratic examiner) speaks *with* contractions — "I've seen enough," "I'll give you one last chance" — and his menace lives entirely in what he says and the flat affect he says it with, never in dropped contractions. Stiffening a "cruelty as procedure" character by stripping his contractions is a misread: the procedural-cruelty mechanic (§4) is about content and delivery, not grammar. **Test:** calibrate against the validated source script's register, not against a plausible-sounding rule about how a "formal" character *should* sound. If the validated reference shows the character using contractions, so do you. A sneering peer like Jin is *more* contractioned, not less ("Seriously? Your parents think some trash they scraped together is gonna fix you?") — the messy jab reads as the character; a cool, writerly one-liner reads as the writer. The failure to watch for is global, not local: a whole script quietly formalized into "did not / could not / I am / it is" across dialogue, narration, AND interior reads like a translated subtitle even when no single line looks broken.

**Sensory defaults.** Tactile first (the locket warm against the chest, the chain on the neck, the air shifting before Loop's telekinesis lands). Visual second, used for geography and social geometry. Auditory under pressure. Smell rarely, and only for setting.

**Interior monologue.** More honest than speech and more specific. Renders other people's calculations as if the protagonist were a forensic witness ("Three hundred ability users had just felt something they couldn't name."). Stakes-as-interior, not emotion-as-interior. Capable of dark wit, but only inwardly.

**Under pressure.** The voice tightens, never loosens. Specificity increases — the protagonist catalogues the threat with more detail, not less. Sentences shorten. Description becomes precise. Verbal output drops, action increases. "Precise rather than panicked" is the core mechanic — fear sharpens observation rather than scattering it.

**At rest.** Quieter. Slight melancholy bleed but never maudlin. One sentence of texture per beat at rest; cut before atmosphere becomes mood-piece.

## §4 Annotated Excerpts — *REFERENCE ONLY · DO NOT IMITATE*

Each excerpt below is a verbatim line-pull from one of the six anchors, followed by an annotation on the **mechanism** it demonstrates. The line itself is welded to a show-specific object (the menu, the locket, the dragon, the cloud) and will not transfer to another promo. The mechanism transfers; the line does not. **Do not lift these lines, their phrasing, their sentence rhythms, or their world-specific objects into other promos.** Read for mechanism; write fresh.

### MECH: Specificity over labels

**GA9436 Pawn Shop (Chris's monologue):**
> "She showed me the menu, Sterling. She kept the menu. She kept it. To laugh at."

What it does: Four sentences. One object (the menu). The cruelty escalates through repetition of the object, not through adjective stacking. The listener can picture the menu in Jenifer's hand, in a drawer, being unfolded for Chris.

**WBT Pearl Crystal (Ren's parents):**
> "If you're still Iron when the semester ends, there's no income. No crystals. And we lose the kitchen too."

What it does: Three sentences, three objects (income, crystals, kitchen). The cruelty here is institutional (Ren's rank decides his family's housing) and the stakes are rendered through the math, not through "my parents will suffer."

### MECH: Cruelty as procedure

**GU9347 Draft Selection (Erin):**
> "There must be a mistake. He's a Level One."

What it does: Four words. No malice, no theater. The narrator immediately names it: *No cruelty in her voice. Just fact. But she'd said it loud enough for three hundred people to hear.* The harm is procedural and the protagonist registers it as world-position, not personal insult.

**WBT Pearl Crystal (Wei):**
> "I've seen enough. Iron. The worst rank in Academy history. Forbidden from climbing the Ascendant Cliffs."

What it does: Five short sentences. Bureaucratic delivery. The third sentence is the world-position; the fourth is the institutional consequence. Wei does not editorialize.

### MECH: Compression at load-bearing moments

**GA9436 Pawn Shop (the locket-voice):**
> "Live, my child."

What it does: Three words. Surrounded by mid-length atmospheric prose (the gold pouring out, the wounds resealing). The compression registers as **decision** — the locket-voice has chosen to speak, has chosen these specific words. If this line had run twelve words long, it would have lost the temple-bell quality the SFX cue is reinforcing.

**WBT Pearl Crystal (Ren to Cloud):** *(⚠ off-essence artifact — "Cloud" is not a canon character; transfer the compression mechanism only, not the spore-as-companion "we")*
> "They don't get to decide who we are anymore. We do."

What it does: Two short sentences. Sits at the climactic moment of the promo — the Pearl Crystal moment — and replaces what a less-good writer would have rendered as a paragraph of internal monologue about identity and worth.

### MECH: The witness who notices

**Princess Ebony (Geralt):**
> "You look wonderful, Princess. Tonight is your night."

What it does: Geralt adjusts the tiara (gesture, §1 Principle 5) and delivers seven words. Two pages later: "You will always be Princess of Allarion to me." — and the fist-to-chest salute. The narrator does not say "Geralt was the only one who saw her clearly." The reader does that inference. This is the tenderness-as-scroll-stopper beat (taxonomy: positive IM beat type, but craft mechanism is restraint).

**GU9347 Draft Selection (Erin):**
> "I know what you are." [BEAT] "A fighter."

What it does: The pause weaponizes the listener's fear (does she know he's a vampire?), then resolves into the witness beat. The compression on "A fighter" carries the whole emotional turn. Without the beat, the line is cheap. With it, it's the scene's load-bearing moment.

### MECH: Character through gesture

**Love Bite (The Mistress):**
> She pressed her thumb to his cheek. She liked doing that.

What it does: One physical action, one sentence of interpretation that is barely interpretation (the narrator names a preference, not an emotion). The reader infers possession, ownership, the protagonist's helplessness, the Mistress's enjoyment. Recurs throughout the scene — by the third time the thumb appears, the listener understands what the Mistress is without the narrator having said anything.

**WBT Pearl Crystal (Ren/Cloud):** *(⚠ off-essence artifact — "Cloud" is not a canon character; the spore can't be fist-bumped or addressed as a companion. Transfer only the gesture-hides-fear mechanism; restage it show-native.)*
> Ren gave his beast a fist bump so he wouldn't see. "Don't worry, Cloud. We've got this."

What it does: The gesture and the dialogue are about Cloud. The narrator clause ("so he wouldn't see") is about Ren's fear. The reader does the inference: Ren is hiding terror from his beast to keep his beast calm. No "Ren was terrified" sentence is needed.

### MECH: System as antagonist

**Princess Ebony (the Officiant):**
> "She will bear his name. She will bear his children. She will not speak in council. She will not hold property. She will not leave his home without permission."

What it does: Five clauses, parallel structure, anaphora. The Officiant is reading from a scroll — the procedure IS the cruelty. The horror is that this is the rule, not a violation of the rule. No villain is needed; the institution is the villain.

**ZW Love Bite (the vampire woman, on the King's treaty):**
> "Aren't we, sweetheart? Your King made an arrangement. We're allowed to hunt one wolf a month inside the city. The treaty."

What it does: The cruelty is delivered as a fact about a legal arrangement. The Lycan King has signed away one of his own subjects per month. There is no villain to defeat here — the harm is the treaty itself. Tristan can kill these two vampires, but next month two more will arrive with the same legal right. The structure persists.

### MECH: Cliff architecture (end on the question)

**Princess Ebony (final beat):**
> "His dragon... Ebony! I know his dragon."

What it does: One line of dialogue from Nova. The narrator then asks four questions in the CTA ("Who is Boris? Why does a vagrant move like a soldier? Why does a beggar carry a dragon?"). The line itself does not answer anything — Nova recognizes the dragon, the listener does not know which dragon, does not know who Boris is, does not know what's about to happen. The promo has initiated the next narrative arc (Nova's recognition) without delivering it.

**GA9436 Pawn Shop (final beat):**
> "Master Sterling. Who else knows you opened it?"

What it does: Sylvia Sterling, the woman from the phone call, has finally arrived. Her first question to Jed is not "who are you" or "how are you feeling" — it's "who else knows." The question implies stakes the listener cannot evaluate (what does opening the locket trigger?), implies a world larger than Jed has seen, and forces the download.

## §5 Universal Banned Patterns (prose-level) — *DRAFTING · REVISION · EVALUATION*

- **Generic snark for the protagonist.** Verbal counter-attack as a default register reads as "writer wrote a line" rather than "character spoke." Sharp comeback lines work only when the surrounding restraint earns them (Quinn's "Better a mistake than the guy who peaks at Level Four" lands because Quinn has been silent for two pages; Ebony's "you'll be the most disappointing person in this castle all by yourself" lands because Edwin has been winning every exchange). The default for the protagonist is restraint; the snap is rare.
- **Stepwise destruction lists.** "Then X. Then Y. Then nothing." Use continuous process — "smaller and smaller fragments, until nothing of it remained."
- **Adverb-tag speech-frames.** "X said quietly / heavily / sharply." Use action-as-tag, or describe voice quality as a complete sentence.
- **"X, [adverb], [position]." constructions.** "Azar, very quietly, from her shoulder." OUT. Replace with action-as-tag or full sentence.
- **Verb-noun mismatches.** "Expression terrified" — the expression doesn't terrify, the face does. Use standard constructions.
- **Metonymy-as-poetry obfuscation.** "Bored mouth" → "bored looking." Don't body-part what an adjective does cleanly.
- **Riddle / decode-required sentences.** Temporal inversions ("the slap landed before the dream did"), premise-as-metaphor ("she'd been poured into a stranger"), and mood-anaphora that withholds the referent ("the hands were wrong. The reach was wrong."). Each makes the listener reconstruct the literal event instead of feeling it — and in audio at ~150 wpm there is no rewind. State the event plainly; let texture ride on top once the line parses. The disorientation can be the character's; the sentence must be clear. This is the banned set for W4 loop Q1. **Q1 also rejects two riddle-cousins that pass a naive scan:** (a) **abstraction for a literal event** — a writerly figure for a concrete mental/physical act ("behind his flat eyes the math ran" → "he counted the odds"; "something in him stood up" → name the act); (b) **rule-as-summary** — a mechanic told as abstract narration ("the genes warred within their tolerances") instead of a plain if/then the listener can act on ("raise one gene five levels above the others and they tear him apart"). Both still make the listener translate; plain-up the same way.
- **Over-elaborated mystical framing.** "For centuries" — one strong image only. Don't stack the elaboration.
- **Long sub-clause sentences with participial chains.** Break into two sentences. **NARRATOR density budget:** default ≤2 sentences per narrator block, ≤1 subordinate/participial clause per sentence. A comma/dash-chained sensory list ("a pit of black stone sunk below the floor, galleries climbing the walls, every bench filled with house colours leaning over the rail") is over-rendered — split it or keep only the load-bearing image. **Scene-entry sentence** states *who + what's in motion* in one clause; atmosphere lands after the first action, never stacked into the opening line ("enter at cost," made countable).
- **Em dashes used as a substitute for restructuring.** Permitted but spare. Default to commas or periods.
- **Labels in place of objects.** "Pathetic." "Loser." "Freak." "Worthless." "Monster." Permitted only when an antagonist is using them as performance (Rex calling Quinn "Talentless Talen" works because the listener has already seen the specific spit-and-laughter cruelty; the label is the antagonist's brand, not the writer's shortcut). Never use as the narrator's voice.
- **Tenderness too early.** Warmth-inside-darkness only lands once the injustice density has been earned. Two scenes of cruelty minimum before the witness beat.
- **Resolution leak.** A promo may initiate resolution but must never deliver its emotional payoff. The payoff lives in the app. This is the resolution-withholding hard requirement (CLAUDE.md → Resolution withholding) at the prose-craft level.
- **Vague-attribution ornament.** "stronger than any whisper had warned," "more than the benches could believe" — an unsourced intensifier dressed as fact. Cut, or replace with the concrete (pairs with §1 Specificity).
- **Suspended-dash reveal.** "And then — a sound behind him." State it plainly: "Then he heard something behind him."
- **Protagonist interiority adopting the antagonist's slur as self-label.** The protagonist's own V.O. calling himself the oppressor's term (livestock / cattle / mutt). The engine is that he *rejects* the label; interiority that owns it is drift. Antagonists may brand him with it; his own thought may not.
- **The "did not" register — un-contracted negative anaphora as a standing rhythm (PERMANENTLY STRUCK).** Repeated un-contracted negatives used as a house cadence: "He did not beg. He did not raise his voice." / "He did not hurry. He did not look up." / "She did not look back." Two failures compound — it strips contractions (§3) *and* runs anaphora, and at ~150 wpm the accumulation reads formal-translated. This is a recurring default across our scripts; strike it on sight. **Rule:** keep at most two or three genuinely load-bearing instances in a whole script (the stage refusal earns one); contract every other one ("He didn't beg," or better, restage the negation as a positive action — what he *did* instead). A stretch with three-plus un-contracted negatives in sequence is an automatic rewrite.

## §6 LLM Failure Modes to Suppress (structural) — *DRAFTING · REVISION · EVALUATION*

Distinct from §5: those are prose-level patterns; these are structural/workflow failure modes that LLM writers default into when not explicitly suppressed. Each is high-frequency and high-impact on funnel retention.

- **Do not start with abstract lore.** No "In the kingdom of Allarion..." / "For centuries, the Lycans ruled..." Open inside a specific perception or with a charged one-line hook. World context rides action.
- **Do not summarize the protagonist's trauma before putting them in motion.** Backstory delivered as exposition before the protagonist DOES anything reads as setup. Drop the protagonist into action; surface backstory through what they're trying to survive.
- **Do not use "little did he know" or any flash-forward narrator wink.** Cancelled pattern. Trust the cliff to do that work.
- **Do not stack adjectives where an object would work.** "His cruel, mocking, sneering smile" → name what he is holding, what he is wearing, what specific thing he says.
- **Do not make villains sound like cartoon bullies.** No mustache-twirling, no "you'll pay for this!" theatrical threats. See §1 Principle 2 — cruelty as common sense.
- **Do not resolve the central conflict before the final CTA/cliffhanger.** The protagonist may win a battle; the war continues. The promo initiates resolution; the app delivers it. See §1 Principle 7 and the `CLIFF-RESOLVES` taxonomy flag.
- **Do not explain emotions immediately after showing them.** If the gesture has landed, the narrator's job is done. "She squeezed his hand. She loved him." → cut the second sentence. See §1 Principle 5.
- **Do not repeat the same humiliation beat more than twice without escalation.** Compounding ≠ repetition. Each cruelty beat must add a new dimension (peer cruelty → institutional sanction → intimate betrayal), not re-stage the same humiliation in a new room.
- **Do not use a twist that creates confusion instead of curiosity.** The cliff's question must be answerable in the listener's head as "I need to download to find out." Not "wait, what just happened?" Confusion kills CTI; curiosity drives it.
- **Do not introduce a new character to solve the scene** unless they are canon or structurally necessary to the engine. Drop-in saviors who appear once to rescue the protagonist break world coherence and read as plot convenience.
- **Do not let texture override clarity.** When a sentence is beautiful but the listener has to re-parse to understand the stakes, cut it. See priority hierarchy in preamble.
- **Do not invent quantitative or timeline specificity.** Years, ages, head-counts, money, dates, distances, tenure ("three years here"). Concrete numbers must be sourced from the W3 beat sheet or world doc — fabricating them to satisfy Principle 1 is hallucination, and frequently a continuity break.
- **Do not pre-explain a mechanic the scene is about to demonstrate.** If the reactions or action will teach the rule (cadets recoiling at "a Level One on the elite squad"), a narrator paragraph explaining the rank/watch system up front is redundant exposition. Let the mechanic ride the action; cut the lecture.
- **Do not muddy genre.** If the show is romantasy, the prose register must read as romantasy; if it's progression fantasy, prose register must read as progression fantasy. Genre confusion in the first 60 seconds destroys CTR. Show-native register lives in the world doc's Essence block.

## §7 Diagnostic Tests — *EVALUATION*

The seven principles in §1 collapse into seven yes/no tests. Each scene should pass at least four; each promo should pass all seven across its full arc. Used by W4 in one-shot clarity pass and in scene-by-scene evaluation.

1. **Specificity test.** Does the scene name at least one concrete object/number/named detail that carries emotional weight? Could a listener describe the object after one hearing?
2. **Procedural cruelty test.** When the antagonist diminishes the protagonist, is the cruelty delivered as fact/common sense rather than as performance? Does the antagonist stay calm?
3. **Compression test.** Is there at least one short utterance (1–6 words) that lands as the scene's load-bearing moment? Is it surrounded by mid-length prose so the compression registers?
4. **Witness test.** Does at least one character (or inner voice, or artifact) register the cost the protagonist won't name aloud? Is the witnessing physical or compressed-verbal, not narrator-interpreted?
5. **Gesture test.** Is at least one character moment delivered through physical action without narrator interpretation? Does the reader do the inference?
6. **System test.** Is the protagonist's harm reinforced by a rule/procedure/institution, not only by individual antagonists? Could the antagonist disappear and the harm continue?
7. **Cliff test.** Does the final 100–200 words narrow to a question the listener cannot answer from the promo alone? Does the prose initiate but not deliver the next arc?

**Clarity gate (always-on — precedes the seven, not one of them).** Read each beat once at audio speed. Every line must land the literal event on first hearing; any line that requires decoding an inversion, a premise-metaphor, or mood-before-fact → revision flag, independent of the seven-test count. Position-independent: applies at line 5 and line 500. This is the evaluation-side mirror of W4 loop Q1. Note it is one-sided toward comprehension at the *line* level; the *opening's* two-sided orientation check (under- vs over-orient) lives in §2 Opening and the W4 Opening Gate.

Beats failing 2+ tests → revision flag in scene-by-scene mode. The one-shot clarity pass runs all seven across the full promo, plus the always-on clarity gate above. The §6 structural failure modes run as a separate scan in the same evaluation pass — each flagged item surfaces for revision regardless of the seven principles' pass count.
